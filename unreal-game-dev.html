Stepping into Unreal Engine: My Indie Game Sandbox

My first deep dive into Unreal's Blueprint system, C++ mechanics, and high-fidelity physics to build a functional prototype from scratch.

April 2026 5 min read

As software engineers, we are accustomed to structured order. We have database tables, well-behaved APIs, and deterministic background threads. But game development is a completely different beast. It’s essentially keeping a high-performance illusion running at exactly 60 (or 120) frames per second, hiding the absolute chaos happening just beneath the surface. And let's be honest, nothing says pure developer dedication like coding custom game physics on a laptop running off a backup inverter while the neighborhood goes pitch black during a load shedding slot.

I wanted to see what it takes to build these interactive worlds. So, I decided to open Unreal Engine 5 and build my own indie mechanics sandbox. The goal? Create a smooth, physics-based movement prototype featuring sliding mechanics, momentum preservation, and a custom grappling hook. What could possibly go wrong?

"In enterprise software, a bug crashes the app. In game development, a bug makes the player clip through the floor, fall through the infinite void, and somehow launch into orbit at the speed of sound."

The Tech Stack Clash: Blueprints vs C++

One of Unreal’s most fascinating architectural choices is its split personality: C++ for heavy lifting and system core architecture, and Blueprints (a visual node-based scripting language) for fast prototyping and high-level gameplay wiring.

Initially, I scoffed at Blueprints. "I'm a backend programmer; I write lines of text, not nodes and wires," I arrogantly thought. I was wrong. Blueprints are incredibly fast for setting up UI, firing audio cues, or handling simple trigger overlaps. However, I quickly realized that if you aren't careful, visual scripting becomes a plate of Italian spaghetti—lines crossing everywhere in an unreadable mess.

I eventually settled on a hybrid architectural pattern: I wrote the core mathematical algorithms, custom movement states, and base physics classes in **C++** using Unreal’s macro system (UPROPERTY, UFUNCTION). Then, I inherited those C++ classes in Blueprints to visually tune numbers, link particle effects, and play animations. It was the perfect separation of concerns.

Conquering Vector Math (and My Sanity)

To make the grappling hook physics feel "juicy," I couldn't rely on simple coordinate interpolation. I needed to apply real forces. I had to dust off my old university physics textbooks and calculate:

  • Tension forces: Restricting the player's distance to the hook anchor point.
  • Centripetal acceleration: Preserving angular momentum to swing around corners beautifully.
  • Friction vectors: Dampening velocity based on the ground angle when transitioning from a swing to a slide.

Writing movement physics in C++ meant dealing with Unreal's tick-based updates. A single wrong vector dot product could turn my smooth player character into an erratic projectile bouncing violently off the walls.

My Key Learnings

  1. Juice is Everything: The absolute cleanest code doesn't matter if the game feels bad to play. Adding subtle details like camera shake, screen tilt during a wall-run, and particle sparks during a slide are what make a mechanic *feel* good. Sound design and feedback loops are just as critical as logic.
  2. Garbage Collection in C++: Unreal C++ is not "raw" C++. It includes a custom garbage collection sweep, reflection headers, and strict class structures. Learning to work within Epic Games' ecosystem was an exercise in understanding specialized, high-performance tooling.
  3. Game Engines are Masterpieces of Systems Engineering: Peeking under the hood of Unreal Engine gave me immense respect for Epic's rendering pipelines, multi-threaded task graphs, and memory management pools. It’s a masterclass in squeezing every drop of power out of hardware.

The Verdict

Tinkering in Unreal Engine didn't just teach me how games are made; it made me a better software engineer. It forced me to think about performance constraints in milliseconds, dive back into mathematical models, and appreciate visual and physical design. The indie sandbox prototype is incredibly fun to jump around in—and it remains my favorite creative playground when I want to build something purely interactive.
